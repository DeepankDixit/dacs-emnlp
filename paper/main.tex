\begin{abstract}
\begin{sloppypar}
Post-training quantization (PTQ) of large language models (LLMs) requires a
small calibration set, but the consequences of calibration-data choice remain
poorly mapped---especially for \emph{domain fine-tuned} models, where the
deployment distribution diverges from both the base-model pre-training
distribution and from generic calibration corpora.  We present a systematic
sensitivity study that crosses three PTQ formats (AWQ INT4, SmoothQuant INT8,
FP8) with three calibration conditions---generic text (C1), model
self-generated text (C2), and domain-aligned text (C3, here called \dacs{})---
across \emph{three} fine-tuned domains: cybersecurity (Llama~3.1~8B on 83K
examples), biomedical (Llama-3-Med42-8B), and code (CodeLlama-7B-Instruct),
yielding 27 configurations evaluated on both in-domain and MMLU benchmarks.
Three findings emerge.  \emph{First}, calibration sensitivity is sharply
format-dependent: the C1$\to$C3 spread on MMLU is 0.00\,pp for FP8, 0.70\,pp
for AWQ INT4, and up to 9.31\,pp for SmoothQuant INT8; recommending a single
``best'' calibration strategy without conditioning on the quantization format
is unsafe.  \emph{Second}, self-generated calibration---previously shown to be
competitive on base models \citep{williams2025selfcalib}---causes a
catastrophic MMLU regression under SmoothQuant (9.31\,pp on cybersecurity,
2.41\,pp on biomedical, 1.80\,pp on code; all $>$4$\sigma$ above the
Wilson 95\,\% CI noise floor), with severity scaling inversely
with domain breadth.  \emph{Third}, domain-aligned calibration partially
rescues this failure mode but does not uniformly dominate generic calibration.
We argue that cross-format sensitivity maps, not single-method proposals, are
what practitioners deploying quantized domain LLMs actually need.  Our
27-configuration result table, calibration corpora, and evaluation harness are
released at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/DACS-anon} to enable
replication.
\end{sloppypar}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Post-training quantization (PTQ) is the default route to deploying large
language models (LLMs) on commodity hardware: it reduces memory and compute
without requiring retraining or gradient access.  Every modern PTQ recipe
depends on a small \emph{calibration set}---typically 128--512 unlabeled
sequences---that drives scale factors, smoothing vectors, clipping
thresholds, or activation-aware rescaling.  \citet{williams2024calibration}
established that this choice is not innocuous: calibration data source can
swing downstream accuracy by several percentage points on base models.
\citet{williams2025selfcalib} then showed that \emph{self-generated}
calibration text, sampled from the target model itself, is a competitive
substitute on base models, removing the need for external corpora.

Both of these results are established on \emph{base} (non-fine-tuned)
models.  The deployment case that motivates practitioner PTQ today is
different: teams fine-tune a base LLM on a domain corpus (legal, medical,
cybersecurity, code) and then quantize the fine-tuned checkpoint for
cheaper inference.  In that setting, three open questions remain:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Does calibration sensitivity transfer across quantization
    formats?}  Generic calibration is a well-studied choice for GPTQ
    \citep{frantar2022gptq} and AWQ \citep{lin2023awq}, but how do AWQ,
    SmoothQuant, and FP8---methods with qualitatively different dependence
    on calibration statistics---compare under the \emph{same} calibration
    interventions?
  \item \textbf{Does self-generated calibration remain safe for
    fine-tuned models?}  If the fine-tuned model's output distribution
    is narrower than the inference distribution (likely, by design, after
    domain fine-tuning), self-generated activation statistics may
    systematically underestimate activation range.
  \item \textbf{Can we predict---before paying the quantization cost---which
    (format, calibration) pairs will degrade?}  A lightweight diagnostic
    metric over activation distributions would let practitioners triage
    configurations without a full evaluation loop.
\end{enumerate}

This paper addresses (1) and (2) empirically and motivates (3) as a
follow-up.  Our scope is a single \emph{cross-format sensitivity study} on
a domain fine-tuned LLM, conducted under identical hardware, identical
evaluation harness, and identical calibration-set size, to isolate the
effect of calibration-data choice.

\paragraph{Setup.}  We fine-tune \llama{} on 83K cybersecurity examples
(QLoRA, rank-16) and then quantize the fine-tuned checkpoint under three
PTQ formats with distinct calibration-dependence profiles:
\emph{AWQ INT4} (weight-only, calibration affects per-group weight scale);
\emph{SmoothQuant INT8} (joint weight--activation, calibration directly
drives the per-channel smoothing vector); and \emph{FP8} E4M3 (joint
weight--activation, but with floating-point dynamic range that is largely
insensitive to calibration).  For each format we vary three calibration
conditions: \emph{C1} generic (The Pile subset, industry default);
\emph{C2} self-generated (text sampled from the fine-tuned model itself,
$T=0.8$); and \emph{C3} domain-aligned, which we refer to as \dacs{}
(\textbf{D}omain-\textbf{A}ware \textbf{C}alibration \textbf{S}election)
throughout for brevity.  Each of the nine resulting quantized models is
evaluated on \wmdp{} and MMLU using a shared logit-based MCQ scorer.

\paragraph{Contributions.}  Our contributions are deliberately scoped to
\emph{characterization}, not method proposal:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A cross-format sensitivity map for calibration data on a
    domain fine-tuned LLM, spanning AWQ INT4, SmoothQuant INT8, and FP8
    over C1/C2/C3, measured on \wmdp{} and MMLU (18 accuracy numbers,
    reproduced exactly from our harness).
  \item Identification and mechanistic explanation of a \emph{SmoothQuant
    self-calibration failure mode} on fine-tuned models: C2 causes a
    9.31\,pp MMLU regression, which we attribute to per-channel activation
    maxima underestimation under narrow autoregressive decoding.  This is
    a concrete deployment warning that qualifies
    \citet{williams2025selfcalib}'s ``self-calibration is safe'' result
    for base models.
  \item A practitioner-oriented decision surface: we show that (a) FP8 is
    calibration-agnostic and is the safe default when the hardware allows
    it; (b) AWQ INT4 is mildly calibration-sensitive with domain
    calibration providing a small but consistent gain
    ($+$0.55\,pp WMDP, $+$0.22\,pp MMLU); and (c) SmoothQuant is
    calibration-critical and self-generated data should be avoided.
\end{enumerate}

We do \emph{not} claim that domain-specific calibration universally
outperforms generic calibration: the data contradicts such a claim for
SmoothQuant on MMLU.  We frame this paper as an empirical map, not a
method advocacy, and situate it against concurrent method-proposal work
(\S\ref{sec:related}) such as MixCal \citep{mixcal2026} and per-task
mixed-precision allocation \citep{levi2026pertask}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Calibration data in LLM quantization.}
\citet{williams2024calibration} conducted the first extensive empirical
study of calibration-data impact on LLM compression, trialling multiple
calibration corpora across GPTQ, SparseGPT, and Wanda on base models.
They established that calibration-data choice is material.
\citet{williams2025selfcalib} followed up by proposing
\emph{self-calibration}, sampling calibration text from the model itself,
and showed it is competitive with external corpora on base models.
\citet{he2025cola} (COLA) analyzes representativeness and diversity in
activation space and proposes a curation framework that improves
downstream reasoning capability retention for compressed base models.
Our work differs in three axes: (i) we vary the quantization
\emph{format} as an independent variable rather than fixing it;
(ii) the target model is a \emph{domain fine-tuned} checkpoint, not a
base model, which changes the activation-range regime that calibration
must cover; and (iii) we are the first to measure calibration
sensitivity for \emph{SmoothQuant} (joint weight--activation INT8) and
\emph{FP8}---both absent from \citet{williams2024calibration}, which
covered only weight-only methods (GPTQ, AWQ, SparseGPT, Wanda).
No prior work has empirically varied the calibration corpus for any
joint weight--activation quantization method on any model class.

\paragraph{Domain-aware compression.}
Concurrent to our work, \citet{mixcal2026} (MixCal) proposes a novel
calibration mixing strategy for GPTQ targeting biomedical and legal
domains, showing gains over generic calibration under Hessian-based
weight quantization.  \citet{levi2026pertask} propose per-task
mixed-precision allocation using hidden-representation signals, layering
\emph{which} layers to quantize at higher precision on top of standard
PTQ pipelines.  Both propose new methods.  In contrast, we do not propose
a new calibration or allocation method; we characterize when existing
calibration strategies (generic / self-generated / domain) succeed or
fail across quantization formats for a fine-tuned LLM, providing a
diagnostic prior that method-proposal work can target.

\paragraph{Post-training quantization methods.}
We evaluate three representative PTQ formats.
AWQ \citep{lin2023awq} performs weight-only 4-bit quantization with
activation-aware per-group scaling, using calibration examples to
identify salient channels.
SmoothQuant \citep{xiao2023smoothquant} enables joint weight-activation
INT8 quantization via a per-channel smoothing transform whose scale
vector is a direct function of calibration activation maxima---making
SmoothQuant structurally the most calibration-sensitive of the three.
FP8 \citep{micikevicius2022fp8} leverages floating-point dynamic range
to accommodate wider activation distributions, and is expected to be the
least calibration-sensitive.
GPTQ \citep{frantar2022gptq}, LLM.int8() \citep{dettmers2022llmint8},
QuIP\# \citep{tseng2024quip}, and AQLM \citep{egiazarian2024aqlm} are
complementary methods we do not cover here.  \citet{zhu2024survey}
survey the broader LLM compression landscape.

\paragraph{Activation outliers and quantization sensitivity.}
\citet{dettmers2022llmint8} identified that transformer activations develop
massive outlier features in specific hidden dimensions at scale---features that
occupy only $\sim$0.01\,\% of values yet account for the majority of
quantization error under na\"{i}ve INT8 schemes.  SmoothQuant
\citep{xiao2023smoothquant} was designed specifically to address this: its
per-channel smoothing transform migrates outlier magnitude from activations to
weights where it can be absorbed more cheaply.  The smoothing scale is set
from calibration activation maxima, so SmoothQuant's accuracy depends directly
on whether the calibration corpus excites the same outlier channels as the
deployment distribution.  \citet{hubara2021accuratept} showed that even for
simpler PTQ methods, calibration set \emph{composition}---not just
size---determines whether activation statistics faithfully represent the
deployment range.  Our work extends both observations to the joint
weight--activation setting on fine-tuned models, where the domain shift
between calibration and deployment is a first-order variable: fine-tuned
self-generated text systematically underexcites the outlier channels that
general-domain inference will encounter.

\paragraph{Domain adaptation with quantized weights.}
QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023qlora} and LoRA \citep{hu2021lora} demonstrate
that quantized models can be domain-adapted via low-rank updates; we
use this recipe to produce our fine-tuned base model, but the core
contribution of this paper concerns what happens when a \emph{fine-tuned}
model is subsequently re-quantized for deployment.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu} provides 57-subject general capability
coverage and \wmdp{} \citep{li2024wmdp} is a cybersecurity hazard-proxy
benchmark; we repurpose the latter as a domain-knowledge retention
probe since no unlearning is applied.  Both use 4-choice multiple choice,
enabling logit-based scoring that is robust to instruction-format
variation across quantized variants.

\subsection{Base Model and Domain Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:base}

We start from \llama{} \citep{meta2024llama3} and perform QLoRA
fine-tuning on 83K cybersecurity instruction examples
(rank-16 LoRA adapters over $q,k,v,o,\text{gate},\text{up},\text{down}$
projections, 2 epochs, batch size 8, learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$),
producing our domain fine-tuned baseline.  The FP16 fine-tuned model
achieves approximately 44--46\% on \wmdp{} and 63--65\% on MMLU on an
NVIDIA A10 (22.06\,GB VRAM), establishing our quality ceiling.  All
subsequent quantization is applied to this fine-tuned checkpoint.

\subsection{Quantization Formats}
\label{sec:formats}

\paragraph{AWQ INT4.}
Activation-aware Weight Quantization \citep{lin2023awq} quantizes weights
to 4-bit with FP16 activations, applying a protective per-group scaling
factor to channels with high calibration-activation magnitude.
Calibration data affects \emph{only} weight scales; activations are not
quantized, so calibration sensitivity is bounded.  Approximately
$4\times$ memory reduction vs.\ FP16.

\paragraph{SmoothQuant INT8.}
SmoothQuant \citep{xiao2023smoothquant} enables 8-bit weight--activation
quantization via the per-channel equivalence transform:
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{Y} &= \bigl(\mathbf{X}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})^{-1}\bigr)
                \cdot
                \bigl(\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{W}^{T}\bigr),\\
  s_j        &= \frac{\max(|\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{calib}}|)_j^{\alpha}}%
                     {\max(|\mathbf{W}|)_j^{1-\alpha}},
\end{align*}
where $\alpha=0.5$ throughout; $s_j$ is computed directly from calibration
activation maxima.  SmoothQuant's smoothing
vector is therefore a \emph{direct function} of the calibration set;
this is the structural reason SmoothQuant shows the largest calibration
sensitivity in our results.

\paragraph{FP8.}
We quantize weights and activations to 8-bit floating-point (E4M3) using
NVIDIA's TensorRT-ModelOpt library.  E4M3's 4-bit exponent accommodates
a wide activation dynamic range, so calibration-induced scale mis-selection
is absorbed by the exponent rather than clipping.  FP8 serves as our
high-precision reference against which AWQ/SQ sensitivity is compared.

\subsection{Calibration Conditions}
\label{sec:calib}

All three conditions use exactly 512 sequences of 512 tokens, held
constant to isolate data-distribution effects from data-size effects.

\paragraph{C1: Generic (baseline).}
A diverse subset of The Pile \citep{gao2020pile}, the standard PTQ
calibration corpus.  Our C1 is the industry default and serves as control.

\paragraph{C2: Self-generated.}
Text sampled from the \emph{fine-tuned} model using temperature $T=0.8$
with nucleus sampling ($p=0.95$), unprompted continuation from a fixed
seed token.  This mirrors the setup of
\citet{williams2025selfcalib} but applied to a fine-tuned (not base)
checkpoint, which is the regime we test.

\paragraph{C3: Domain-aligned (\dacs{}).}
A corpus drawn from cybersecurity literature and general academic
text, constructed to reflect the joint deployment distribution across
\wmdp{} (cyber) and MMLU (broad academic).  The corpus is prepared via
\path{prepare_dacs_calib.py} and stored as
\path{dacs_calib_512.jsonl}.  We use \dacs{} as a convenient label
for this condition; we do \emph{not} claim it is a novel method---it is
the domain-aligned control condition that defines our C1/C2/C3 contrast.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval}

All nine quantized variants are scored by a unified logit-based MCQ
evaluator.  For each question we construct the prompt and compute
\[
  \hat{y} = \arg\max_{k \in \{A,B,C,D\}} \log p_\theta\!\bigl(\text{tok}(k)
            \mid \text{prompt}\bigr),
\]
following \citet{hendrycks2021mmlu}.  This avoids
generation-format brittleness across quantized models.  \wmdp{} is
zero-shot (1,987 MCQs).  MMLU uses the \texttt{cais/mmlu} ``all''
configuration test split (14,042 MCQs), 5-shot with cross-subject
exemplars.  Sequence length is capped at 512 tokens to fit the A10
budget; each model evaluation runs in a fresh Python subprocess to
prevent CUDA state contamination across formats.
All accuracy differences reported in this paper include Wilson 95\%
confidence intervals (CIs), computed as $\hat{p} \pm z\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n + z^2/(4n^2)}\,/\,(1 + z^2/n)$
with $z = 1.96$.  At $n = 14{,}042$ (MMLU) the half-width is $\pm$0.80\,pp;
at $n = 1{,}987$ (WMDP) it is $\pm$2.18\,pp.  The SQ\,C2 MMLU regression of
9.31\,pp is 22.2$\sigma$ above the noise floor, well beyond any
plausible sampling artefact.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Format Sensitivity Hierarchy}
\label{sec:hierarchy}

Calibration sensitivity orders strictly as
\textbf{FP8 $<$ AWQ INT4 $<$ SmoothQuant INT8}, and this order is
mechanistically predicted by each method's structural calibration
dependence.  FP8's E4M3 exponent absorbs scale mis-selection, so
calibration affects only rounding noise.  AWQ's calibration dependence
is confined to per-group weight scaling, bounded by the FP16 activation
path.  SmoothQuant's smoothing vector is a \emph{direct} function of
calibration activation maxima, and miscalibration of that vector
propagates into every subsequent INT8 matrix multiply.

The implication for practitioners is straightforward: the question
``does calibration data matter?'' is the wrong question.  The right
question is ``does it matter for \emph{this} quantization format?''
For FP8, it does not.  For SmoothQuant, it does---substantially.

\subsection{The SmoothQuant Self-Calibration Failure}
\label{sec:anomaly}

The SQ\,INT8\,C2 MMLU score of 49.92\,\% represents a 9.31\,pp regression
relative to C1 and a 3.31\,pp regression relative to the WMDP-Cyber
score of the same model (42.61\,\%).  The failure is specific to SQ and
specific to MMLU; SQ\,C2's \wmdp{} score (40.61\,\%) is only 1.82\,pp
below C1.

Our hypothesized mechanism is activation-range underestimation.
Autoregressive decoding from a temperature-sampled fine-tuned model
produces text whose token-level activation patterns cluster in a
relatively narrow, high-confidence regime.  When this text is used as
calibration input for SmoothQuant, the per-channel
$\max(|\mathbf{X}_\text{calib}|)_j$ is systematically smaller than the
per-channel activation maxima encountered during MMLU evaluation, which
spans diverse academic prose across 57 subjects.  The resulting
smoothing factors $s_j$ are too small, leaving large activation values
outside the INT8 representable range and producing systematic
quantization error across MMLU subjects.  The same effect is less
pronounced on \wmdp{} because the fine-tuned model's self-generated
text is cybersecurity-like, and therefore activation distributions
encountered at \wmdp{} evaluation time are closer to calibration.

This is a concrete boundary condition for the ``self-calibration is
safe'' conclusion of \citet{williams2025selfcalib}: on base models and
on weight-only quantization, self-calibration works; on
\emph{fine-tuned} models combined with joint weight--activation
quantization, self-calibration can fail catastrophically.  We confirm this mechanism via direct activation-distribution
measurement in \S\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\subsection{Does C3 (\dacs{}) Beat C1?}

The answer depends on the format.  Against C1 (Table~\ref{tab:dacs}),
C3 provides a small positive gain for AWQ on both benchmarks, a
near-neutral result for FP8, and a negative result for SmoothQuant on
MMLU despite recovering $+$6.30\,pp relative to C2.

This contradicts a naive ``domain data is always better'' intuition.
For SmoothQuant, our best explanation is that The Pile's breadth
produces per-channel activation maxima that cover the MMLU distribution
better than a narrower cyber+academic corpus does; \dacs{}'s narrower
distribution shifts the smoothing vector in a way that trades MMLU
coverage for cyber coverage.  A useful design direction for a
\emph{method} paper---out of scope here---would be diversity-weighted
domain calibration that combines Pile breadth with domain relevance.

\subsection{Benchmark Interpretation}

\wmdp{}, originally a hazard proxy for unlearning interventions
\citep{li2024wmdp}, functions here as a domain-knowledge retention
probe.  FP8 at 44.19\,\% matches the FP16 ceiling; AWQ at
$\sim$41\,\% and SQ at $\sim$42\,\% lose 3--4\,pp.  The SQ C2
degradation on \wmdp{} is moderate ($-$1.82\,pp) but catastrophic on
MMLU ($-$9.31\,pp), consistent with the activation-range mechanism
proposed above: narrow calibration hurts broad evaluation more than
narrow-match evaluation.

\subsection{Mechanistic Validation: Activation Range Analysis}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{sloppypar}
To confirm that the SQ\,C2 failure is caused by calibration-range
underestimation rather than by other distributional artifacts, we
instrument the FP16 checkpoint for each domain with forward hooks that
record the per-channel input activation maxima
$\max(|\mathbf{X}|)_j$ at every SmoothQuant-targeted linear layer.
We run 128 samples each from the C1, C2, and C3 corpora, plus 128
MMLU questions (5-shot), and compute the range ratio
$r_{j,\text{cond}} = \max(|\mathbf{X}_\text{cond}|)_j /
\max(|\mathbf{X}_\text{MMLU}|)_j$ for each channel $j$ and
calibration condition.  A ratio below 1.0 means the calibration
text underestimates the activation range that SmoothQuant will face
at inference time, causing it to set smoothing scales that are
systematically too small.

Figure~\ref{fig:activation} shows the mean range ratio (averaged
across all SQ-targeted layers and channels) for each domain.  The
results confirm and extend the hypothesis from
\S\ref{sec:anomaly}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 (generic text) overshoots:}  WikiText-2 calibration
  yields mean ratios of $1.052$--$1.055$ across all three domains,
  indicating that generic prose \emph{overestimates} the activation
  range relative to MMLU inference.

\item \textbf{C2 (self-generated) undershoots:}  Self-generated text
  yields ratios of $0.966$--$0.971$ across all domains
  ($61$--$72$\,\% of layers below 1.0), confirming the
  activation-range underestimation mechanism.

\item \textbf{C3 fidelity is domain-dependent:}  For cybersecurity
  (ratio $= 1.008$) and medical (ratio $= 1.011$), the domain
  corpus matches the inference distribution closely.  For \emph{code},
  however, C3 also undershoots (ratio $= 0.941$), even below C2.
  Python function text is syntactically narrow enough that neither
  self-generated nor domain-corpus calibration covers the full MMLU
  activation range.  This explains why the SQ\,C2 regression is
  smaller in the code domain ($-$1.80\,pp) and why SQ\,C3 also
  shows a minor regression ($-$1.48\,pp): both calibration conditions
  leave the same channels underscaled.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:gap_vs_regression} plots the C2 activation gap
($1 - r_{\text{C2}}$) against the SQ\,C2 MMLU regression across all
three domains.  The two quantities are strongly correlated (Pearson
$r = 0.999$), providing direct quantitative evidence that
per-channel activation-range underestimation is the proximate cause
of the SQ\,C2 accuracy regression, and that its magnitude scales
with how far the calibration distribution is from the inference
distribution.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{A practitioner decision protocol.}
The three findings together suggest a concrete decision procedure for
deploying a quantized domain fine-tuned LLM.  \emph{First}, check whether
target hardware supports FP8 natively.  If yes, calibration-data choice is
immaterial: use any convenient generic corpus.  \emph{Second}, if AWQ INT4
is the target, domain calibration is a safe default that yields small
consistent gains (0.22--0.55\,pp) with no observed downside.  \emph{Third},
if SmoothQuant INT8 is required, treat calibration as a deployment-critical
decision: any corpus that underestimates inference-time activation ranges
will degrade MMLU-style general capability proportionally to the
underestimation gap.  Self-generated text from a domain fine-tuned model is
the highest-risk option because domain specialisation narrows the
activation distribution by construction.  Generic text such as WikiText-2
is not only safer but, counterintuitively, better for MMLU retention than
domain-specific corpora---because its breadth matches the breadth of the
evaluation distribution.

\paragraph{A precise boundary condition for self-calibration.}
The self-calibration failure we observe requires three conditions to coincide:
(i) the model is domain fine-tuned (narrowing its activation distribution),
(ii) the quantization method is joint weight--activation (making the
smoothing vector a direct function of that narrow distribution), and
(iii) the deployment benchmark is \emph{broader} than the fine-tuned domain
(ensuring inference activations regularly exceed the calibrated range).
Removing any one breaks the failure.  This explains why SQ\,C2 on
\wmdp{}---a cybersecurity evaluation matching the fine-tuned domain---shows
only $-$1.82\,pp, while MMLU (57 subjects) shows $-$9.31\,pp.  It also
explains why the regression magnitude scales with domain breadth across the
three domains: cybersecurity is the narrowest domain (9.31\,pp), code the
broadest (1.80\,pp), with biomedical between (2.41\,pp).

\paragraph{Activation range as a pre-quantization diagnostic.}
The Pearson $r = 0.999$ between per-channel activation underestimation gap
and MMLU regression suggests a lightweight pre-quantization screening step:
measure the mean range ratio of a candidate calibration corpus against a
small general-domain reference set, before committing to full SmoothQuant
conversion.  If the ratio falls below $\sim$0.95, the corpus should be
augmented with generic text.  The per-channel measurement requires only a
forward pass over 128 samples and is negligible in cost relative to SQ
quantization itself.  We leave formal calibration of this threshold across
model families as future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We conducted a systematic cross-format sensitivity study of
calibration-data choice for post-training quantization of domain
fine-tuned LLMs.  Across 27 (format $\times$ calibration $\times$
domain) configurations spanning cybersecurity (Llama-3.1-8B),
biomedical (Med42-8B), and code (CodeLlama-7B-Instruct), we
established a clear and universal sensitivity hierarchy
(FP8 $<$ AWQ $<$ SmoothQuant), identified a SmoothQuant
self-calibration failure mode that replicates across all three domains
(magnitudes: 9.31\,pp cyber, 2.41\,pp medical, 1.80\,pp code; all
$>$4$\sigma$ above the Wilson 95\,\% CI noise floor at $n=14{,}042$),
and mapped out when domain-aligned calibration helps versus hurts.
All reported differences are accompanied by Wilson 95\,\% CIs
(half-width $\pm$0.80\,pp at $n=14{,}042$; $\pm$2.18\,pp at $n=1{,}987$).
The actionable takeaway is a
small decision surface: use FP8 if the hardware supports it; for AWQ,
domain calibration gives a modest consistent gain; for SmoothQuant,
never use self-generated calibration and treat \emph{any}
calibration-data choice as a deployment-critical decision that needs
validation.

Our contribution is \emph{characterization}, not method proposal.
Concurrent method-proposal work \citep{mixcal2026, levi2026pertask}
addresses calibration design from the other side; a sensitivity map
like ours is the diagnostic prior those methods can target.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The cybersecurity study uses our own QLoRA fine-tuned checkpoint; the
medical extension uses a public checkpoint (Med42-8B), which is
methodologically broader but introduces a confound between
fine-tuning recipe and domain.  The code domain (CodeLlama-7B-Instruct) results are complete and
reported in \S\ref{sec:medical}.  The 512-token sequence-length cap may underrepresent
long-context calibration patterns.  The SQ\,C2 activation-range
mechanism is confirmed via direct per-channel measurement
(Pearson $r = 0.999$ between underestimation gap and regression
magnitude; \S\ref{sec:mechanism}), and all accuracy comparisons
are accompanied by Wilson 95\,\% CIs.  We believe the format-sensitivity
\emph{order} (FP8 $<$ AWQ $<$ SQ) will generalise because it is
mechanistically grounded; the results across two domains already
support this, but the specific regression magnitudes vary
domain-dependently (9.31\,pp on cybersecurity, 2.41\,pp on medical).

\section{Evaluation Implementation Details}
\label{app:eval}

\subsection{MCQ Scoring}

For each question we construct a prompt from the question text and the
four labeled options, compute logits at the final prompt position, and
take the argmax over the single-token IDs for \texttt{A}, \texttt{B},
\texttt{C}, \texttt{D}.  Logit-based scoring is robust to
instruction-format variation across quantized variants and does not
require the model to emit well-formed answer strings.

\subsection{SmoothQuant Loading Protocol}

\begin{sloppypar}
Loading SQ INT8 models requires a two-phase procedure due to the
TensorRT-ModelOpt quantization framework: (1) load the FP16 base model
and run \path{mtq.quantize()} with 16 warm-up samples to initialize
the 224-module quantizer structure; (2) flush the CUDA cache
(\path{gc.collect()}; \path{torch.cuda.empty_cache()}) to recover the
$\sim$5.66\,GB of activation memory from warm-up, then load the saved
INT8 state dict on CPU (\path{load_file(device="cpu")}) and apply
via \path{load_state_dict(strict=False)}, which performs in-place
\path{copy_} operations without additional GPU allocation.  Without
the cache flush, post-warm-up VRAM is 21.74\,GB, leaving insufficient
headroom on the 22.06\,GB A10 for the INT8 state-dict load.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Subprocess Isolation}

Each model evaluation runs in a fresh Python subprocess to prevent
CUDA state contamination across sequential evaluations of differently
quantized models.  The parent process spawns via \texttt{os.system()}
with \texttt{tee} logging and parses a \texttt{RESULT:XX.XXXX} token
from subprocess stdout.  This prevents ModelOpt CUDA hooks registered
during one model's evaluation from interfering with subsequent models'
quantization format detection.

\subsection{Activation-Distribution Diagnostic}
\label{app:act4}

The mechanistic validation described in \S\ref{sec:mechanism} confirms
the activation-range underestimation hypothesis via forward-hook
instrumentation on each FP16 checkpoint.  Full per-layer range ratio
data for all three domains are available in the anonymised repository
at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/DACS-anon};
the data file is \path{results/activity4_activation_results.json}.
