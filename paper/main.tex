\begin{abstract}
\begin{sloppypar}
Post-training quantization (PTQ) of large language models (LLMs) requires a
small calibration set, but the consequences of calibration-data choice remain
poorly mapped, especially for \emph{domain fine-tuned} models, where the
deployment distribution diverges from both the base-model pre-training
distribution and from generic calibration corpora.  We present a systematic
sensitivity study that crosses three PTQ formats (AWQ INT4, SmoothQuant INT8,
FP8) with three calibration conditions: generic text (C1), model
self-generated text (C2), and domain-aligned text (C3, here called \dacs{}),
across \emph{three} fine-tuned domains: cybersecurity (Llama~3.1~8B on 83K
examples), biomedical (Llama-3-Med42-8B), and code (CodeLlama-7B-Instruct),
yielding 27 configurations evaluated on both in-domain and MMLU benchmarks.
Three findings emerge.  \emph{First}, calibration sensitivity is sharply
format-dependent: the max calibration spread on MMLU is 0.00\,pp for FP8,
up to 1.03\,pp for AWQ INT4, and up to 9.08\,pp for SmoothQuant INT8;
recommending a single ``best'' calibration strategy without conditioning on
the quantization format is unsafe.  \emph{Second}, self-generated
calibration, previously shown to be competitive on base models
\citep{williams2025selfcalib}, causes a substantial MMLU regression under
SmoothQuant on every domain tested (9.08\,pp on cybersecurity, 2.41\,pp on
biomedical, 1.80\,pp on code; all $>$3$\sigma$ above the Wilson 95\,\% CI
noise floor), with severity scaling inversely with domain breadth.  The
failure is SmoothQuant-specific, however: for AWQ on the biomedical Med42
checkpoint, self-generated calibration is in fact the \emph{best} MedQA
condition ($+$3.20\,pp vs.\ generic), underscoring that calibration choice
must be jointly conditioned on format and model.  \emph{Third}, domain-aligned
calibration partially rescues this failure mode but does not uniformly
dominate generic calibration.
We argue that cross-format sensitivity maps, not single-method proposals, are
what practitioners deploying quantized domain LLMs actually need.  Our
27-configuration result table, calibration corpora, and evaluation harness are
released at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/DACS-anon} to enable
replication.
\end{sloppypar}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Post-training quantization (PTQ) is the default route to deploying large
language models (LLMs) on commodity hardware: it reduces memory and compute
without requiring retraining or gradient access.  Every modern PTQ recipe
depends on a small \emph{calibration set}, typically 128--512 unlabeled
sequences, which drives scale factors, smoothing vectors, clipping
thresholds, or activation-aware rescaling.  \citet{williams2024calibration}
established that this choice is not innocuous: calibration data source can
swing downstream accuracy by several percentage points on base models.
\citet{williams2025selfcalib} then showed that \emph{self-generated}
calibration text, sampled from the target model itself, is a competitive
substitute on base models, removing the need for external corpora.

Both of these results are established on \emph{base} (non-fine-tuned)
models.  The deployment case that motivates practitioner PTQ today is
different: teams fine-tune a base LLM on a domain corpus (legal, medical,
cybersecurity, code) and then quantize the fine-tuned checkpoint for
cheaper inference.  In that setting, three open questions remain:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Does calibration sensitivity transfer across quantization
    formats?}  Generic calibration is a well-studied choice for GPTQ
    \citep{frantar2022gptq} and AWQ \citep{lin2023awq}, but how do AWQ,
    SmoothQuant, and FP8, methods with qualitatively different dependence
    on calibration statistics, compare under the \emph{same} calibration
    interventions?
  \item \textbf{Does self-generated calibration remain safe for
    fine-tuned models?}  If the fine-tuned model's output distribution
    is narrower than the inference distribution (likely, by design, after
    domain fine-tuning), self-generated activation statistics may
    systematically underestimate activation range.
  \item \textbf{Can we predict, before paying the quantization cost, which
    (format, calibration) pairs will degrade?}  A lightweight diagnostic
    metric over activation distributions would let practitioners triage
    configurations without a full evaluation loop.
\end{enumerate}

This paper addresses (1) and (2) empirically and motivates (3) as a
follow-up.  Our scope is a single \emph{cross-format sensitivity study} on
a domain fine-tuned LLM, conducted under identical hardware, identical
evaluation harness, and identical calibration-set size, to isolate the
effect of calibration-data choice.

\paragraph{Setup.}  We fine-tune \llama{} on 83K cybersecurity examples
(QLoRA, rank-16) and then quantize the fine-tuned checkpoint under three
PTQ formats with distinct calibration-dependence profiles:
\emph{AWQ INT4} (weight-only, calibration affects per-group weight scale);
\emph{SmoothQuant INT8} (joint weight--activation, calibration directly
drives the per-channel smoothing vector); and \emph{FP8} E4M3 (joint
weight--activation, but with floating-point dynamic range that is largely
insensitive to calibration).  For each format we vary three calibration
conditions: \emph{C1} generic (WikiText-2, industry default);
\emph{C2} self-generated (text sampled from the fine-tuned model itself,
$T=0.8$); and \emph{C3} domain-aligned, which we refer to as \dacs{}
(\textbf{D}omain-\textbf{A}ware \textbf{C}alibration \textbf{S}election)
throughout for brevity.  Each of the nine resulting quantized models is
evaluated on \wmdp{} and MMLU using a shared logit-based MCQ scorer.

\paragraph{Contributions.}  Our contributions are deliberately scoped to
\emph{characterization}, not method proposal:
(1) a cross-format sensitivity map (AWQ INT4 / SmoothQuant INT8 / FP8
$\times$ C1/C2/C3) on a domain fine-tuned LLM, replicated across three
domains for 27 total configurations;
(2) identification and mechanistic explanation of a SmoothQuant
self-calibration failure mode on fine-tuned models (9.08\,pp MMLU
regression on cybersecurity), attributed to per-channel activation
underestimation under narrow autoregressive decoding, a deployment
warning that qualifies \citet{williams2025selfcalib}'s
``self-calibration is safe'' result on base models;
(3) a practitioner decision surface: FP8 is calibration-agnostic and
the safe default when hardware allows; AWQ INT4 is mildly sensitive
with domain calibration providing small consistent gains
($+$0.55\,pp WMDP, $+$0.22\,pp MMLU); SmoothQuant is
calibration-critical and self-generated data must be avoided.
We do \emph{not} claim domain calibration universally outperforms
generic calibration: the data contradicts that for SmoothQuant on
MMLU.  We situate this work against concurrent method-proposal work
(\S\ref{sec:related}) including MixCal \citep{mixcal2026} and
per-task mixed-precision allocation \citep{levi2026pertask}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Calibration data in LLM quantization.}
\citet{williams2024calibration} first established that calibration-data
choice is material for LLM compression across GPTQ, SparseGPT, and Wanda
on base models. \citet{williams2025selfcalib} then proposed
\emph{self-calibration} (sampling calibration text from the target model)
as competitive with external corpora on base models, and
\citet{he2025cola} (COLA) curates calibration sets for diversity and
representativeness in activation space.  All three studies fix the
quantization method and vary the corpus; all three target base models.
Our work differs in three axes: (i) the quantization \emph{format} is an
independent variable, (ii) the target model is a \emph{domain fine-tuned}
checkpoint, and (iii) we report the first calibration-sensitivity
results for SmoothQuant (joint weight--activation INT8) and FP8,
neither covered by prior calibration-data studies.

\paragraph{Concurrent method-proposal work.}
\citet{mixcal2026} (MixCal) proposes a calibration mixing strategy for
GPTQ on biomedical and legal domains; \citet{levi2026pertask} propose
per-task mixed-precision allocation from hidden-representation signals.
Both propose new methods; we instead characterise when existing
calibration strategies succeed or fail, providing a diagnostic prior
those method-proposal approaches can target.

\paragraph{PTQ formats and activation outliers.}
The three formats we evaluate are AWQ (weight-only 4-bit with
activation-aware scaling; \citealp{lin2023awq}), SmoothQuant (joint
W8A8 via a per-channel smoothing transform whose scale is a direct
function of calibration activation maxima; \citealp{xiao2023smoothquant}),
and FP8 E4M3 (floating-point with a 4-bit exponent absorbing scale
variation; \citealp{micikevicius2022fp8}).  GPTQ
\citep{frantar2022gptq}, LLM.int8() \citep{dettmers2022llmint8},
QuIP\# \citep{tseng2024quip}, and AQLM \citep{egiazarian2024aqlm} are
complementary methods we do not evaluate; \citet{zhu2024survey} survey
the broader landscape.  The outlier-feature analysis of
\citet{dettmers2022llmint8} and the calibration-composition argument of
\citet{hubara2021accuratept} jointly motivate SmoothQuant's high
sensitivity: when self-generated calibration text underexcites the
outlier channels that general-domain inference activates, the smoothing
scales SmoothQuant learns are systematically too small.

\paragraph{Domain adaptation and evaluation.}
QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023qlora} and LoRA \citep{hu2021lora} give us the
fine-tuned base model; the core question is what happens when that
fine-tuned model is subsequently re-quantized.  MMLU
\citep{hendrycks2021mmlu} provides 57-subject general-capability
coverage; \wmdp{} \citep{li2024wmdp} is a cybersecurity-knowledge probe
(repurposed here from its original unlearning context); both use
4-choice MCQ format, enabling logit-based scoring robust to
instruction-format variation across quantized variants.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

An overview of the experimental pipeline (FP16 fine-tuning $\to$
$3 \times 3$ calibration--format matrix $\to$ dual-benchmark
evaluation) is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:pipeline},
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}.

\subsection{Base Model and Domain Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:base}

We start from \llama{} \citep{meta2024llama3} and perform QLoRA
fine-tuning on 83K cybersecurity instruction examples (rank-16 LoRA
adapters over $q,k,v,o,\text{gate},\text{up},\text{down}$ projections,
2 epochs, batch size 8, learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$).  The FP16
fine-tuned model achieves approximately 44--46\% on \wmdp{} and
63--65\% on MMLU, establishing our quality ceiling.  All experiments run on a single Lambda A10 (24\,GB); end-to-end
reproduction of the full 27-configuration table from base checkpoints
costs approximately \$50 of cloud compute (${\sim}1$ GPU-day) at
current rates.  All subsequent quantization is applied to this fine-tuned
checkpoint.

\subsection{Quantization Formats}
\label{sec:formats}

We evaluate three formats with distinct calibration-dependence profiles.
\textbf{AWQ INT4} \citep{lin2023awq} quantizes weights to 4-bit with
FP16 activations and a calibration-driven per-group scaling factor;
calibration affects only weight scales (bounded sensitivity).
\textbf{SmoothQuant INT8} \citep{xiao2023smoothquant} quantizes both
weights and activations to INT8 via the per-channel equivalence
transform $\mathbf{Y} = (\mathbf{X}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})^{-1})
\cdot (\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{W}^{T})$ with
$s_j = \max(|\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{calib}}|)_j^{\alpha} /
\max(|\mathbf{W}|)_j^{1-\alpha}$ ($\alpha=0.5$); since $s_j$ is a
\emph{direct} function of calibration activation maxima, SmoothQuant
is structurally the most calibration-sensitive of the three.
\textbf{FP8} (E4M3) \citep{micikevicius2022fp8} quantizes both
weights and activations to 8-bit floating-point; the 4-bit exponent
absorbs scale variation, so calibration-induced mis-selection
becomes rounding noise rather than clipping.  We use NVIDIA's
TensorRT-ModelOpt implementations throughout.

\subsection{Calibration Conditions}
\label{sec:calib}

All three conditions use exactly 512 sequences of 512 tokens, held
constant to isolate data-distribution effects from data-size effects.

\paragraph{C1: Generic (baseline).}
WikiText-2 \citep{merity2017wikitext}: 512 cleaned-Wikipedia passages,
identical across all three quantization formats and all three domains.
WikiText-2 is the reference calibration corpus used in the original AWQ
release \citep{lin2023awq}; we apply it uniformly to AWQ, SmoothQuant,
and FP8 so that any difference in downstream accuracy is attributable
to the format--calibration interaction rather than to a calibration-corpus
shift across formats.  C1 is our generic-prose control.

\paragraph{C2: Self-generated.}
Text sampled from the \emph{fine-tuned} model using temperature $T=0.8$
with nucleus sampling ($p=0.95$), unprompted continuation from a fixed
seed token.  This mirrors the setup of
\citet{williams2025selfcalib} but applied to a fine-tuned (not base)
checkpoint, which is the regime we test.

\paragraph{C3: Domain-aligned (\dacs{}).}
A corpus drawn from cybersecurity literature and general academic
text, constructed to reflect the joint deployment distribution across
\wmdp{} (cyber) and MMLU (broad academic).  The corpus is prepared via
\path{prepare_dacs_calib.py} and stored as
\path{dacs_calib_512.jsonl}.  We use \dacs{} as a convenient label
for this condition; we do \emph{not} claim it is a novel method, it is
the domain-aligned control condition that defines our C1/C2/C3 contrast.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval}

All nine quantized variants are scored by a unified logit-based MCQ
evaluator.  For each question we construct the prompt and compute
\[
  \hat{y} = \arg\max_{k \in \{A,B,C,D\}} \log p_\theta\!\bigl(\text{tok}(k)
            \mid \text{prompt}\bigr),
\]
following \citet{hendrycks2021mmlu}.  This avoids
generation-format brittleness across quantized models.  \wmdp{} is
zero-shot (1,987 MCQs).  MMLU uses the \texttt{cais/mmlu} ``all''
configuration test split (14,042 MCQs), 5-shot with cross-subject
exemplars.  Sequence length is capped at 512 tokens to fit the A10
budget; each model evaluation runs in a fresh Python subprocess to
prevent CUDA state contamination across formats.
All accuracy differences reported in this paper include Wilson 95\%
confidence intervals (CIs), computed as $\hat{p} \pm z\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n + z^2/(4n^2)}\,/\,(1 + z^2/n)$
with $z = 1.96$.  At $n = 14{,}042$ (MMLU) the half-width is $\pm$0.80\,pp;
at $n = 1{,}987$ (WMDP) it is $\pm$2.18\,pp.  The SQ\,C2 MMLU regression of
9.08\,pp is 22.3$\sigma$ above the noise floor, well beyond any
plausible sampling artefact.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Format Sensitivity Hierarchy}
\label{sec:hierarchy}

The order \textbf{FP8 $<$ AWQ INT4 $<$ SmoothQuant INT8} is
mechanistically predicted by each method's structural calibration
dependence: FP8's E4M3 exponent absorbs scale mis-selection (rounding
noise only); AWQ's dependence is confined to per-group weight scaling
behind a FP16 activation path; SmoothQuant's smoothing vector is a
\emph{direct} function of calibration activation maxima, propagating
miscalibration into every INT8 matrix multiply.  The right question
for practitioners is not ``does calibration data matter?'' but
``does it matter for \emph{this} format?'' For FP8 no, for
SmoothQuant substantially.

\subsection{The SmoothQuant Self-Calibration Failure}
\label{sec:anomaly}

The SQ\,INT8\,C2 MMLU score of 49.92\,\% represents a 9.08\,pp regression
relative to C1 (59.00\,\%).  Strikingly, on the in-domain \wmdp{}
benchmark the same SQ\,C2 model matches SQ\,C1 exactly
(both 40.61\,\%; $\Delta = 0.00$\,pp): calibration choice on a
cyber-flavoured evaluation is invisible despite being decisive on
MMLU.  This is exactly what the activation-range mechanism predicts
when the calibration distribution matches the evaluation
distribution (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).

Our hypothesized mechanism is activation-range underestimation.
Autoregressive decoding from a temperature-sampled fine-tuned model
produces text whose token-level activation patterns cluster in a
relatively narrow, high-confidence regime.  When this text is used as
calibration input for SmoothQuant, the per-channel
$\max(|\mathbf{X}_\text{calib}|)_j$ is systematically smaller than the
per-channel activation maxima encountered during MMLU evaluation, which
spans diverse academic prose across 57 subjects.  The resulting
smoothing factors $s_j$ are too small, leaving large activation values
outside the INT8 representable range and producing systematic
quantization error across MMLU subjects.  The same effect is less
pronounced on \wmdp{} because the fine-tuned model's self-generated
text is cybersecurity-like, and therefore activation distributions
encountered at \wmdp{} evaluation time are closer to calibration.

This is a concrete boundary condition for the ``self-calibration is
safe'' conclusion of \citet{williams2025selfcalib}: on base models and
on weight-only quantization, self-calibration works; on
\emph{fine-tuned} models combined with joint weight--activation
quantization, self-calibration can fail catastrophically.  We confirm this mechanism via direct activation-distribution
measurement in \S\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\subsection{Does C3 (\dacs{}) Beat C1?}

The answer depends on the format (Table~\ref{tab:main} $\Delta$
column).  C3 gives a small positive gain for AWQ on both benchmarks
($+$0.55 \wmdp{}, $+$0.22 MMLU), is neutral for FP8, and for
SmoothQuant trades MMLU breadth for in-domain coverage ($+$1.56
\wmdp{}, $-$2.78 MMLU; though recovering $+$6.30\,pp relative to
SQ\,C2 on MMLU).  This contradicts a naive ``domain data is always
better'' intuition: WikiText-2's encyclopaedic breadth produces
per-channel activation maxima that cover MMLU better than a
narrower cyber+academic corpus, while \dacs{}'s tighter distribution
matches cyber-flavoured activations more faithfully:
diversity-weighted hybrid calibration is a natural method-paper
follow-up that we leave out of scope.

\subsection{Mechanistic Validation: Activation Range Analysis}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{sloppypar}
To confirm the SQ\,C2 failure is caused by calibration-range
underestimation, we instrument each FP16 checkpoint with forward
hooks recording per-channel activation maxima at every
SmoothQuant-targeted linear layer.  Each calibration corpus and a
matched MMLU sample (128 sequences each) yield a per-channel
\emph{range ratio} $r_{j,\text{cond}} =
\max(|\mathbf{X}_\text{cond}|)_j /
\max(|\mathbf{X}_\text{MMLU}|)_j$; ratios below 1.0 indicate
SmoothQuant will set smoothing scales that are systematically too
small.  Full protocol in Appendix~\ref{app:act4}.

Figure~\ref{fig:activation} shows the mean range ratio per condition
and domain.  The pattern is decisive: WikiText-2 (C1)
\emph{overshoots} inference range across all three domains
(ratios $1.052$--$1.055$), while self-generated calibration (C2)
systematically \emph{undershoots}, most catastrophically on cyber
(ratio $0.788$, gap $0.212$, $100\,\%$ of layers below 1.0), less so
on medical ($0.966$, $61.6\,\%$) and code ($0.971$, $71.9\,\%$).
Domain calibration (C3) closely matches inference for cyber and
medical (ratios $1.008$, $1.011$) but also undershoots for code
($0.941$): Python function text is syntactically narrow enough that
neither self-generated nor domain-corpus calibration covers the full
MMLU activation range, which explains the residual SQ\,C3 code
regression ($-$1.48\,pp).

Per-layer analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:act4},
Fig.~\ref{fig:gap_per_layer}) resolves the aggregate observation
across the $n = 224$ SmoothQuant-targeted linear modules per domain.
The C2 underestimation gap distributions are cleanly separated:
cyber median $0.153$ (IQR\,$[0.131, 0.268]$), medical $0.015$, code
$0.007$, with cyber's lower quartile alone exceeding medical and
code upper quartiles.  The failure is a consistent property of the
calibration mismatch across the architecture, not an artifact of
a few outlier layers.
\end{sloppypar}

\paragraph{SQ C2 self-calibration regression replicates, at a

fine-tuned with a broader recipe than our narrow QLoRA cybersecurity
adapter, resulting in less activation-distribution narrowing under
self-generated decoding.  This confirms that the failure mode is not
an artefact of our specific fine-tuning setup; it generalises, but
that its severity scales with the degree of domain narrowing.

accuracy.  Interestingly, for AWQ, C2 outperforms C3 on MedQA
($+$3.20\,pp vs.\ $+$1.80\,pp vs.\ C1); we attribute this to
Med42's self-generated text being strongly biomedical (more domain-aligned
than our PubMedQA-based C3 corpus), a nuance that reinforces the
complexity of calibration-data selection and strengthens the case for
cross-format sensitivity maps over single-method recommendations.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Practitioner decision surface.}
Our findings imply a concrete deployment procedure.  If target
hardware supports FP8 natively, calibration-data choice is immaterial.
For AWQ INT4, domain calibration is a safe default with small
consistent gains (0.22--0.55\,pp).  For SmoothQuant INT8, calibration
is deployment-critical: any corpus that underestimates inference-time
activation ranges degrades MMLU-style general capability
proportionally to the gap.  Self-generated text from a domain
fine-tuned model is the highest-risk option, because domain
specialisation narrows the activation distribution by construction;
generic text such as WikiText-2 is both safer and, counterintuitively,
better for MMLU retention than domain-specific corpora: its breadth
matches the breadth of the evaluation distribution.

\paragraph{Boundary condition for self-calibration.}
The failure requires three conditions to coincide: (i) a domain
fine-tuned model (narrow activation distribution), (ii) a joint
weight--activation quantization method (smoothing vector is a
direct function of that distribution), and (iii) a deployment
benchmark broader than the fine-tuned domain (inference activations
exceed the calibrated range).  Removing any one breaks the failure,
which is why SQ\,C2 on \wmdp{} (cyber-matched) shows 0.00\,pp
while MMLU (57 subjects) shows $-$9.08\,pp, and why regression
magnitude scales with domain breadth (cyber 9.08, medical 2.41,
code 1.80\,pp).

\paragraph{Pre-quantization diagnostic.}
The cleanly-separated per-layer C2 underestimation distributions
(\S\ref{sec:mechanism}, Fig.~\ref{fig:gap_per_layer}) suggest a
cheap screening step: measure the per-layer activation range ratio
of a candidate calibration corpus against a small general-domain
reference set before committing to SmoothQuant conversion.  As an
empirical threshold from our data, if the median per-layer ratio
falls below $\sim$0.95 (gap $> 0.05$), augment with generic text.
The measurement is a single forward pass over 128 samples.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Across 27 configurations spanning cybersecurity, biomedical, and code
fine-tuned LLMs, calibration sensitivity orders strictly as
FP8 $<$ AWQ INT4 $<$ SmoothQuant INT8 and the order is
mechanistically grounded in each format's structural calibration
dependence.  SmoothQuant's self-calibration failure on domain
fine-tuned models replicates across all three domains
(9.08 / 2.41 / 1.80\,pp MMLU regression, all $>3\sigma$ above the
Wilson 95\,\% CI noise floor) with severity scaling inversely with
domain breadth.  The actionable surface: use FP8 if hardware allows;
for AWQ, domain calibration gives a modest consistent gain; for
SmoothQuant, never use self-generated calibration.  Our contribution
is characterisation, not method proposal; a sensitivity map
concurrent method-proposal work (\citealp{mixcal2026};
\citealp{levi2026pertask}) can target.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The cybersecurity study uses our own QLoRA fine-tuned checkpoint; the
medical and code extensions use public checkpoints (Med42-8B,
CodeLlama-7B-Instruct), introducing two confounds.  First, fine-tuning
recipe and domain are entangled: Med42's broader instruction tuning
differs from our narrow QLoRA cybersecurity adapter, and either
factor could plausibly contribute to the smaller SQ\,C2 regression we
observe on medical (2.41\,pp vs.\ 9.08\,pp on cybersecurity).  Second,
CodeLlama-7B-Instruct is a 7B model rather than 8B, so the apparent
scaling of SQ\,C2 regression magnitude with domain narrowness
(9.08 $>$ 2.41 $>$ 1.80\,pp) is not fully separable from model size.
In-domain knowledge retention for the code domain is not measured in
this study: HumanEval is a generation benchmark and is incompatible
with our logit-based MCQ scorer, so we report MMLU only for code, and
treat cross-format sensitivity (rather than absolute capability) as the
quantity of interest there.  The 512-token sequence-length cap may
underrepresent long-context calibration patterns.  MedQA results
($n=500$) carry a Wilson 95\,\% half-width of $\pm$4.34\,pp, so MedQA
deltas below that threshold should be read as descriptive rather than
inferential.  The SQ\,C2 activation-range mechanism is confirmed via
direct per-layer measurement: the cyber per-layer underestimation gap
distribution (median 0.153, $n = 224$ SmoothQuant-targeted layers) is
cleanly non-overlapping with the medical (median 0.015) and code
(median 0.007) distributions; the rank order of these distributions
matches the rank order of the corresponding SQ\,C2 MMLU regressions
(\S\ref{sec:mechanism}), and all accuracy comparisons in the main
tables are accompanied by Wilson 95\,\% CIs.  We believe the format-sensitivity \emph{order}
(FP8 $<$ AWQ $<$ SQ) will generalise because it is mechanistically
grounded; the results across three domains support this, but the
specific regression magnitudes vary domain-dependently
(9.08\,pp cyber, 2.41\,pp medical, 1.80\,pp code).

\paragraph{Ethical Considerations.}
This work evaluates post-training quantization of publicly available
language models on publicly released benchmarks; no human-subjects
research was conducted and no new sensitive data was collected.
\wmdp{} \citep{li2024wmdp} is a hazard-proxy benchmark originally
designed for unlearning evaluation; we repurpose it here as a
multiple-choice domain-knowledge retention probe with no unlearning
intervention applied, consistent with the benchmark's release license.
Quantization broadly improves the accessibility of LLM inference by
reducing memory and compute requirements; the calibration-sensitivity
characterization we provide is intended to help practitioners deploy
quantized domain models reliably.  Generative AI assistance was used
for writing assistance (paper editing, figure-script drafting) and
coding (debugging, anonymisation sweeps) throughout the project; all
research design, experimental methodology, analyses, and claims are
the authors' own.

\subsection{MCQ Scoring}

For each question we construct a prompt from the question text and the
four labeled options, compute logits at the final prompt position, and
take the argmax over the single-token IDs for \texttt{A}, \texttt{B},
\texttt{C}, \texttt{D}.  Logit-based scoring is robust to
instruction-format variation across quantized variants and does not
require the model to emit well-formed answer strings.

\subsection{SmoothQuant Loading Protocol}

\begin{sloppypar}
Loading SQ INT8 models requires a two-phase procedure due to the
TensorRT-ModelOpt quantization framework: (1) load the FP16 base model
and run \path{mtq.quantize()} with 16 warm-up samples to initialize
the 224-module quantizer structure; (2) flush the CUDA cache
(\path{gc.collect()}; \path{torch.cuda.empty_cache()}) to recover the
$\sim$5.66\,GB of activation memory from warm-up, then load the saved
INT8 state dict on CPU (\path{load_file(device="cpu")}) and apply
via \path{load_state_dict(strict=False)}, which performs in-place
\path{copy_} operations without additional GPU allocation.  Without
the cache flush, post-warm-up VRAM is 21.74\,GB, leaving insufficient
headroom on the 22.06\,GB A10 for the INT8 state-dict load.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Subprocess Isolation}

Each model evaluation runs in a fresh Python subprocess to prevent
CUDA state contamination across sequential evaluations of differently
quantized models.  The parent process spawns via \texttt{os.system()}
with \texttt{tee} logging and parses a \texttt{RESULT:XX.XXXX} token
from subprocess stdout.  This prevents ModelOpt CUDA hooks registered
during one model's evaluation from interfering with subsequent models'
quantization format detection.

\subsection{Activation-Distribution Diagnostic: Full Protocol}
\label{app:act4}

This appendix expands the mechanistic validation summarised in
\S\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\paragraph{Instrumentation.}
For each domain's FP16 checkpoint we install forward hooks on every
linear layer targeted by SmoothQuant (q/k/v/o, gate, up, down across
all 32 transformer blocks: 224 layers per checkpoint).  The hooks
record the per-channel input activation maxima
$\max(|\mathbf{X}|)_j$.  We pass 128 sequences each from the C1, C2,
and C3 calibration corpora, plus 128 MMLU questions (5-shot,
matched-length), and compute the per-channel range ratio
$r_{j,\text{cond}} = \max(|\mathbf{X}_\text{cond}|)_j /
\max(|\mathbf{X}_\text{MMLU}|)_j$.  A ratio below 1.0 means the
calibration text underestimates the activation range SmoothQuant
will face at inference time, causing smoothing scales that
are systematically too small.

\paragraph{Aggregate findings (per condition).}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 (generic text) overshoots:}  WikiText-2 calibration
  yields mean ratios of $1.052$--$1.055$ across all three domains,
  indicating that generic prose \emph{overestimates} the activation
  range relative to MMLU inference.
\item \textbf{C2 (self-generated) undershoots in all three domains,
  with magnitudes that scale with the narrowness of the fine-tuning
  distribution:}  cyber C2 mean ratio $= 0.788$ ($100\,\%$ of layers
  below 1.0), medical C2 $= 0.966$ ($61.6\,\%$), code C2 $= 0.971$
  ($71.9\,\%$).  The cyber C2 ratio is the most extreme
  calibration-vs-inference mismatch we observe.
\item \textbf{C3 fidelity is domain-dependent:}  For cybersecurity
  (ratio $= 1.008$) and medical (ratio $= 1.011$), the domain
  corpus matches the inference distribution closely.  For \emph{code},
  however, C3 also undershoots (ratio $= 0.941$), even below C2:
  Python function text is syntactically narrow enough that neither
  self-generated nor domain-corpus calibration covers the full MMLU
  activation range.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Per-layer distribution.}
Figure~\ref{fig:gap_per_layer} shows the per-layer C2 underestimation
gap distribution per domain.  Each domain contributes $n = 224$
independent SmoothQuant-targeted linear modules (32 transformer
layers $\times$ 7 projection types).  The cyber median gap is
$0.153$ (IQR\,$[0.131, 0.268]$), more than $10\times$ the medical
median ($0.015$; IQR\,$[-0.006, 0.035]$) and $20\times$ the code
median ($0.007$; IQR\,$[-0.001, 0.056]$).  Cyber's lower quartile
alone exceeds the medical and code upper quartiles.  The rank
ordering of per-layer distributions exactly matches the rank
ordering of SQ\,C2 MMLU regressions ($9.08 / 2.41 / 1.80$\,pp),
providing distribution-level (rather than $n=3$ aggregate) evidence
that the underestimation is a consistent architectural property of
the calibration mismatch rather than an aggregate driven by a few
outlier layers.
